The Same Three Tricks Keep Getting You to Click

16 September 2026

Pop culture clickbait has settled into a rut, and it works because the patterns are older than most of the people clicking. The first trick is the false choice. "This Actor Was a Star. Then She Made One Decision That Ended Everything." You know the decision is just a normal career move, but the framing makes it feel like a fork in the road. The second is the numbered list with a twist. "10 Films That Bombed. Number 7 Will Make You Angry." You scroll for the anger, not the information. The third is the celebrity name jammed next to an emotion you don't expect. "Tom Hanks Cries on Live TV. The Reason Is Not What You Think." It always is what you think.

These patterns persist because they target a specific gap in your attention. You have a spare minute, your thumb is hovering, and the headline offers a small hit of certainty. You think you're about to learn something secret, but you're really about to confirm what you already suspected. That's the engine. The click is never about the content. It's about the feeling of being let in on something, even when the something is thin.

The platforms know this. They've tested every headline against every other headline, and the same three shapes keep winning. So they keep serving them up. The result is a culture that talks about pop culture through a filter of manufactured stakes. Every film is a disaster or a triumph. Every actor is a comeback or a fall. Nuance doesn't survive the headline.

You can't stop the feed, but you can stop pretending the click is your choice. It's a reflex, trained by repetition. The only way out is to notice the pattern before you act on it. Ask yourself if the headline is offering information or just a shape you've seen a hundred times. Most days, it's the shape.
